How the training data was prepared

The model needs to learn differences such as bright versus dull or sharp versus soft without confusing those differences with the type of object being struck. We prepared the data so that it learns from real sound differences while still keeping CLAP's general knowledge of audio.

Starting with real impact sounds

The main source is RealImpact, a collection of everyday objects being struck in a controlled recording setup. The objects are made from ceramic, metal, plastic, and wood. They were struck at different places and recorded from different positions, so the collection contains natural changes in brightness, attack, and ringing time.

RealImpact provides 10,000 distinct impact recordings after selecting one microphone channel for each strike. We keep 1,500 recordings from 34 objects with reliable material labels. The curated set is balanced evenly across the four materials, with 375 recordings per material.

The final model uses these real recordings as they were captured. It does not train on artificially brightened, dulled, sharpened, or lengthened versions. Earlier experiments explored those edits, but they are not part of the model reported on this website.

Keeping objects separate

We divide the collection by physical object, not by individual recording. Once an object is placed in training, validation, or testing, every recording of that object stays in the same group. This prevents the model from hearing one recording of an object during training and then being tested on another recording of the same object.

This makes the test harder and more realistic. The model must apply what it learned to objects it has never heard before.

Turning each sound into three listening qualities

For every recording, we first find the moment when the object is struck. We then measure three qualities that sound designers commonly listen for.

QualityMeaning in everyday listeningHow it is measured
Brightnesswhether the sound feels dull or brighthow much of its energy sits in higher frequencies
Attackwhether the strike begins softly or sharplyhow quickly it rises from the start to its peak
Decaywhether the sound is short and dry or keeps ringinghow long its energy takes to fall after the strike

If a property cannot be measured reliably, that recording is not used to teach that property. The missing value is never guessed.

Comparing like with like

Brightness does not mean the same thing for every material. Even a dull metal strike may contain more high-frequency energy than a bright wooden strike. A single threshold for the whole collection would mostly teach the model to identify the material.

Instead, each recording is compared with other recordings from the same material. A bright ceramic sound is bright relative to other ceramic sounds. A bright wooden sound is bright relative to other wooden sounds. The same rule is used for attack and decay.

All ranges used for these comparisons are calculated from the training group only. Information from validation and testing is not used to define what counts as high or low.

Teaching through real comparisons

The model learns by comparing natural recordings. For example, it may see two ceramic impacts and learn which one is brighter, which one begins more sharply, or which one rings for longer. Comparisons are only used when the measured difference is clear enough to be meaningful.

Decay is also learned from natural recordings. We do not create artificial ringing tails because changing a tail can also change the apparent brightness and attack. Using the real recordings avoids teaching the model from an edit that changes several qualities at once.

What was checked before training

1

Known material

Only objects with a reliable material label are used for the material-based comparisons.

2

Reliable measurement

A recording teaches a quality only when that quality can be measured clearly from the waveform.

3

Clear difference

Two sounds are compared only when their measured difference is large enough to provide a useful training signal.

4

No object overlap

Recordings from the same physical object never appear on both sides of the training and evaluation split.

Keeping CLAP's broader knowledge

RealImpact teaches the model about fine differences between impact sounds, but training on that collection alone can make it worse at general audio search. We therefore mix three kinds of examples in every training batch.

Training sourceAvailable recordingsShare of each batchWhat it teaches
RealImpact80050%fine differences in brightness, attack, and decay
Clotho80025%matching everyday audio with natural-language captions
FSD50K80025%recognising a broad range of sound categories

There are 2,400 available training recordings in total. RealImpact makes up half of each batch even though the three sources contain the same number of recordings. This gives fine-grained impact supervision more weight while every batch still includes general audio examples.

The model learns fine detail and general audio meaning at the same time. CLAP itself remains frozen. Only the small ResiCLAP branches and their gates are trained.

What this curation is designed to prevent

The preparation steps stop the model from taking easy shortcuts. It cannot decide that metal always means bright, memorise an object that later appears in testing, treat an uncertain measurement as truth, or improve fine-grained search by forgetting how ordinary audio and text relate.
