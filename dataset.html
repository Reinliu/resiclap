The data

Teaching a model to hear a single property requires pairs of sounds that differ in that property and nothing else. RealImpact supplies the recordings; a curation pipeline supplies the contrast.

Source recordings

RealImpact is a set of real recordings of everyday objects struck at controlled points and captured by a dense microphone array. Every recording carries its object, material, strike point and microphone position, which is what makes queries possible that differ only in acoustic detail rather than in category.

Training and test splits are separated by object, so no object heard during training appears at test time. The held-out set contains unseen objects — Mug (ceramic), Bin (plastic), Flowerpot (small ceramic), Vase (wood), Spatula (metal) — recorded across multiple strike points, microphone distances and angles.

Whether the model still works as a general audio search engine is measured separately on Clotho and FSD50K, two standard audio–text datasets.

1. Measuring the three properties

Objects are grouped into materials by name, and every clip is analysed after detecting the moment of the strike. Three properties are measured:

PropertyWhat is measured
Brightnessthe centre of gravity of the spectrum over the first second after the strike
Attackhow quickly the sound rises to its peak
Decayhow long the ring takes to fall by 20 dB

Clips whose ring never falls by 20 dB before the recording ends are excluded from decay supervision rather than guessed at.

2. Making the targets material-relative

A ceramic bowl is brighter than a wooden block in absolute terms even when it is the dullest ceramic in the set, so an absolute threshold would simply re-encode which material it is. Each property is therefore scored within its own material, using statistics computed on the training split only. A clip in the bottom of its material's range is labelled low, one in the top high, and the middle band is left unlabelled. Splits are assigned at the object level, so neither an object nor a normalisation statistic ever crosses from training into test.

3. Controlled variants

Natural recordings rarely give two clips of the same object differing in exactly one property. Each reference clip is therefore processed into candidates pushed toward the low and high end of its own material's range: a shelving filter to brighten or dull, and time-warping or a transient boost to sharpen or soften the attack. Every candidate is loudness-matched back to the reference, so loudness can never be the cue that separates them.

Processing strength is not fixed in advance. All candidates are generated, re-measured, and then filtered — so a dull ceramic receives a different push than an already-bright one.

4. What a candidate must survive

A candidate is kept only if it clears every gate below.

1

Headroom

The original must have room to move. A clip already at the bright end of its material cannot be brightened further.

2

Direction

The re-measured property must actually move the intended way. Intent is never assumed from the setting used.

3

Plausibility

The result must still land inside the range spanned by real recordings of that material.

4

No leakage

The other two properties must barely move. This is the gate that makes a pair genuinely single-property.

Finally, every accepted variant must stay close to its original in frozen CLAP's own embedding space, so no variant drifts far enough to change what the clip is a recording of.

5. Why decay has no synthetic variants

Decay is supervised only by natural recordings. Synthetic decay variants were built in an earlier pass and then discarded: lengthening or truncating a ring changes how bright and how sharp the clip measures as well, so the pairs were never single-property. Rather than train on supervision we could not keep clean, decay pairs are drawn from natural recordings whose ring time already differs under controlled geometry.

This is a deliberate asymmetry rather than an oversight, and it shows in the results: brightness and attack benefit from controlled augmentation, decay does not.
