How the training data was prepared

The model needs to learn differences such as bright versus dull or sharp versus soft without confusing those differences with the type of object being struck. We prepared the data so that it learns from real sound differences while still keeping CLAP's general knowledge of audio.

Starting with real impact sounds

The main source is RealImpact, a collection of everyday objects being struck in a controlled recording setup. The objects are made from ceramic, metal, plastic, and wood. They were struck at different places and recorded from different positions, so the collection contains natural changes in brightness, attack, and ringing time. RealImpact was introduced by Clarke and colleagues at CVPR 2023.1

Keeping objects separate

We divide the collection by physical object, not by individual recording. Once an object is placed in training, validation, or testing, every recording of that object stays in the same group. This prevents the model from hearing one recording of an object during training and then being tested on another recording of the same object.

This makes the test harder and more realistic. The model must apply what it learned to objects it has never heard before.

Measuring each recording

A 5 ms moving average follows the loudness of the waveform. The strike begins when this curve reaches 10% of its peak. All three qualities are then measured from the waveform rather than labelled by a listener.

QualityExact measurement
BrightnessSpectral centroid over the active sound. This is the frequency balance point of the recording. A higher value sounds brighter.
AttackTime from the detected onset to the first point that reaches 90% of the local envelope peak. A shorter rise sounds sharper.
DecayT20, the time taken for the backward-integrated sound energy to fall by 20 dB. A longer time means more ringing.

If a property cannot be measured reliably, that recording is not used to teach that property. The missing value is never guessed.

Comparing sounds within the same material

Different materials naturally occupy different frequency ranges, so brightness must be judged relative to the material. For example, a dull metal strike can still contain more high-frequency energy than a bright wooden strike. If one brightness threshold were applied to the whole collection, the labels would mostly separate metal from wood instead of separating bright from dull sounds within each material.

Instead, each recording is compared with other recordings from the same material. A bright ceramic sound is bright relative to other ceramic sounds. A bright wooden sound is bright relative to other wooden sounds. The same rule is used for attack and decay.

All ranges used for these comparisons are calculated from the training group only. Information from validation and testing is not used to define what counts as high or low.

Brightness curation

Brightness candidates are made with a second-order high-shelf filter whose corner is fixed at 2 kHz. Four strengths are tried in each direction.

DirectionFilter settingsWhat it does
Brighter+3, +6, +9, and +12 dBraises the frequencies above the 2 kHz shelf
Duller−3, −6, −9, and −12 dBreduces the frequencies above the 2 kHz shelf

The filter leaves the low-frequency body and timing envelope largely unchanged. After filtering, the candidate is matched back to the original recording's RMS level so that extra loudness cannot reveal which version is brighter.

Attack curation

Attack candidates are made in two ways. The first changes the speed of the rise from onset to the local peak while leaving the later tail aligned. The second reshapes only the beginning of the sound.

DirectionTime-warp settingsOnset-shaping settings
Sharper0.5, 0.25, 0.125, and 0.0625a transient boost of 1, 2, or 4 with a 2 ms exponential fall
Softer1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.5, and 5.0a sine-squared fade-in lasting 5, 10, or 20 ms

The onset shaping is limited to the first 40 ms. Every candidate is RMS-matched to the original and peak-limited to 0.98 before it is measured again.

How a processed candidate is accepted

The processing setting alone is not treated as proof that the sound changed correctly. Every candidate is measured again and must pass all four checks below.

1

Room to change

A high candidate is attempted only below the material's 90th percentile. A low candidate is attempted only above its 10th percentile.

2

Correct direction

The new measurement must actually move toward bright or dull, or toward sharp or soft.

3

Still realistic

The result must remain between the 1st and 99th percentiles of real recordings from the same category.

4

Other qualities stay put

The edit is rejected if it changes a quality that was not meant to move.

Edited qualityMaximum allowed change in brightnessMaximum allowed change in attackMaximum allowed change in decay
Brightnessnot applicable0.150.15
Attack0.10not applicable0.15

When several settings pass, the pipeline keeps the candidate closest to the target point. High aims for the 75th percentile and low aims for the 25th percentile within the same category.

Decay curation

There is no accepted synthetic decay transform. Extending a tail also raises room noise and often changes brightness because high frequencies fade faster than low frequencies. Shortening a tail can also alter the measured attack. Those edits failed the requirement that only one quality should change.

Current method: natural decay comparisons

The accepted decay data therefore comes from natural recordings. The object, material, and strike point stay fixed while microphone distance or angle changes. The recordings are measured again using T20 and kept only when they form a clear short, medium, and long ringing sequence.

How shorter synthetic decay could be created

Shortening is the safer direction because it removes existing tail energy rather than inventing new sound. Two candidate families can be tested:

  • Apply exponential damping after a protected onset window, using time constants of 30, 60, 120, or 250 ms.
  • Apply a raised-cosine tail fade beginning 50, 100, or 200 ms after onset.

The protected onset window keeps the initial strike unchanged. Each candidate must then be RMS-matched, peak-limited to 0.98, measured again, and rejected if brightness or attack changes by more than 0.15.

How longer synthetic decay could be created

Lengthening is harder because the missing tail has to be generated. The most faithful option is modal resynthesis. The dominant ringing frequencies are fitted separately, then each frequency's damping time is multiplied by 1.5, 2, or 3 before the sound is reconstructed. Treating each frequency separately matters because high frequencies usually fade faster than low frequencies.

A longer candidate would be accepted only if its T20 increases in the intended direction, it remains inside the natural decay range of its category, and both brightness and attack move by no more than 0.15. It must also pass a listening check for phasing, metallic ringing, and amplified room noise. These synthetic decay settings are a proposed extension and are not used in the reported model.

How this relates to the reported model

The processing recipe above defines the controlled curation and the hard-limit listening examples used on the Demo page. The final model reported in the Results table is trained on the natural object-held-out RealImpact recordings rather than on the processed brightness and attack variants. This distinction keeps the website aligned with the reported experiment.
